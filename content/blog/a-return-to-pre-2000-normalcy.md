Australian house prices have outrun income and rent for the last three decades. The relationship between prices, income and rent was treated as obsolete. Prices are now falling. This exercise asks how far they would have to fall to return to the pre-2000 relationship, using the 1996 trough as the destination.

Two measures do the work. Price-to-income compares the house price index with household disposable income per capita. Price-to-rent compares the same price index with rent, the investment earnings of the dwelling. They sit near the top of their history, but the rent measure needs a larger fall to reach its 1996 trough.

## The measures

Price-to-income divides the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) nominal house price index by Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) household gross disposable income per capita. The income is seasonally adjusted and divided by estimated resident population. The ratio is indexed to 1996 = 100.

Disposable income is after tax and transfers, before housing costs. It includes rent received by household landlords and excludes corporate, institutional and foreign investors' income. The series begins in 1981.

The measure is per capita. Population is a stable denominator over the long run. Household formation responds to house prices, so population is the cleaner base for a long series. This is a relative price measure. It is not a household affordability measure.

Price-to-rent divides the same house price index by the OECD rent price index. The ratio is the inverse of the gross rental yield. It is indexed to 1996 = 100. A higher index means more years of gross rent to buy the dwelling. The series begins in 1972.

The gross measure is the usual long-run comparison. A net cap rate after vacancy, operating costs and depreciation would be lower. The ranking is similar if the expense ratio is broadly stable over long periods, which this exercise assumes.

## Where we are

| Measure         | Latest | Date    | Percentile |
| --------------- | ------ | ------- | ---------- |
| Price-to-income | 200.6  | 2026-Q1 | 100th      |
| Price-to-rent   | 267.6  | 2025-Q3 | 98th       |

Price-to-income is at its series high. Price-to-rent is about 6% below its 2022 peak. Both sit near the top of their history.

## Why the old relationship

The pre-2000 index levels are the reference because the rules changed after that period. Higher rates are the historical norm. The low-rate period after 2000 was the anomaly. The fall in rates fuelled leverage throughout the financialised economy. Most of the price shift came from that leverage. Lower interest rates made debt cheaper and raised borrowing capacity. Financial deregulation let lenders and borrowers act on that capacity. The two reinforced each other, and household debt grew much faster than income from the 1990s. Negative gearing and the 50% capital gains tax discount (from 1999) amplified demand for investment property, but they were secondary to the fall in rates. The exercise uses the pre-2000 relationship because it is the last period before the stretch. That is the sense in which it is normalcy. It is the old regime. It is not a natural constant.

## The destinations

Both measures need a fall to their 1996 trough: 50% for price-to-income and 63% for price-to-rent. The income series' 1983 minimum is a deeper stress case, 57% below today. It is a single data point, so it is not a destination.

The 1996 trough is the last major trough before the current high, and the common destination for both measures. The rent series' all-time low is 1972, before the figure's 1981 start.

Price-to-income is about 2.0 times its 1996 trough. Against the 1983 minimum, it is 2.3 times, but that is a single observation. Price-to-rent is about 2.7 times its 1996 trough. The rent measure is more stretched.

## Time does part of the work

A fall is not the only path. With nominal prices flat:

| Measure         | Assumption                        | Years to the trough |
| --------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Price-to-income | disposable income +3.5% a year    | 20                  |
| Price-to-rent   | rents +4% a year                  | 25                  |
| Price-to-income | 30% fall, then flat, income +3.5% | 10                  |

The income measure needs about 20 years of flat prices, or a 30% fall and 10 years of income growth, to return to its 1996 trough. The rent measure needs about 25 years of flat prices at 4% rent growth, because the gap to its 1996 trough is larger.

## Caveats

Prices, income and rents are nominal. The indices are inflation-neutral. In the ladder, income and rents are held at their current levels; the time estimates state their growth assumptions. The exercise measures distance in today's prices.

The exercise treats the historical relationships as a useful guide. It does not require prices to return there. The trough is a reference point. The likely mechanism is debt: the price falls relative to income and rent when credit stops expanding or becomes more expensive.

The data is quarterly and published with a lag. Price-to-income ends 2026-Q1 and price-to-rent ends 2025-Q3.

## They will print

The exercise asks how far prices would have to fall to return to the pre-2000 relationship. The answer is a long way.

If anything remotely close to this scenario occurs, it is this author's opinion that the government will print the difference.

Policy is currently resisting a fall by pushing the cost onto younger buyers, as argued in [Dousing the Pyre](/blog/dousing-the-pyre/). Younger buyers' mortgages at today's prices let existing owners sell and lenders keep the loans performing.

It is also priming the fall: the 2026-27 Budget abolishes negative gearing for established homes from 1 July 2027 for properties bought after 12 May 2026, and replaces the 50% CGT discount with cost-base indexation and a 30% minimum tax. The two pull in opposite directions, but the post-2000 low-rate regime is the historical anomaly behind the distortion of property prices.

If the government turns to monetisation instead, the price level rises. The debt is fixed in nominal terms, so its real value falls. But low rates and repression can also filter into financialised assets, including investment housing, which bids up prices and worsens the ratios. The effect on the ratios is ambiguous. But one would hope, for the next generation, that this time they go down and stay down.

The numbers above are the nominal-fall scenario. They are an exercise and do not forecast the future.
